Yuki Kanayama

I am a PhD candidate in Economic Geography at the London School of Economics. Before joining LSE in 2024, I worked for Japan’s Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, where I was involved in regulatory reform in the construction industry and international cooperation on railway projects. I received my B.A. (2018) and M.A. (2020, valedictorian) in Economics from Waseda University, Japan.

My research interests include empirical studies in urban economics, particularly on housing supply constraints. I have been working on housing dereliction in a superstar city (Tokyo) and how lot geometry contributes to land market frictions (Tokyo). My current projects include the determinants of housing consumption (England) and the role of voluntary vacancies as an endogenous housing supply constraint (Japan). I am also interested in ambiguous property boundaries as a source of land market illiquidity.

My supervisors are Christian Hilber and Felipe Carozzi.

Apart from research, I also enjoy running, visiting museums and listening to music in the vein of UK/US indie rock and 90s Swedish pop.

Research

Publications and Working Papers

  • Housing dereliction in a superstar city: Speculative neglect and its disamenity effects 2026

    Kanayama, Y. and Sadayuki, T. LSE Geography and Environment Discussion Paper Series: Papers in Economic Geography and Spatial Economics, No.59. | Media coverage: Toyo Keizai (東洋経済)

    Abstract

    Why do derelict houses persist in urban areas despite strong demand? What are their effects on neighbourhoods? This paper proposes a simple mechanism — speculation over future real estate value increases — to explain dereliction amid prosperity and to quantify its disamenity effects. First, we develop a dynamic discrete choice model showing that expectations of future urban regeneration can incentivise property owners to delay redevelopment, thereby prolonging dereliction. Using parcel-level GIS data from central Tokyo, we find that properties located in areas designated for future regeneration are 7–16 percentage points more likely to be derelict. Second, we estimate the effect of derelict houses on nearby rents using future regeneration as an instrument — affecting current rents only through dereliction. Our 2SLS results show that one additional derelict house within 80 metres reduces rents by 1.6% on average. The estimated effect is magnified to 3.6–6.5% in areas with low accessibility to public safety services, suggesting that renters’ concerns about fire and crime risks amplify the disamenity effects. Our findings indicate that rational forward-looking behaviour can reduce effective housing supply and generate negative neighbourhood externalities, pointing to unintended consequences of place-based urban policies.

  • What types of houses remain vacant? Evidence from a municipality in Tokyo, Japan 2021

    Kanayama, Y. and Sadayuki, T. Journal of the Japanese and International Economies, 62, 101167.

    Abstract

    Preventing the occurrence of vacant houses and reutilizing existing vacant houses are two known ways of addressing housing vacancies. This study uses parcel-level data on single-family houses in the municipality of Toshima in Tokyo, Japan, to examine determinants of the occurrence and reutilization of vacant houses. The data reveal that, conditional on land prices, houses with restricted and costly redevelopment opportunities are more likely to become vacant. The results suggest that owners with such properties disregard the redevelopment potential and, as a result, tend to have a high reservation price relative to the market value. Consequently, owners face difficulty finding a transaction partner and even become reluctant to engage in a deal (i.e., they withdraw from the market). The results also suggest that revitalization of the neighborhood community may contribute to activating vacant houses. Local governments can use an empirical assessment such as this to implement efficient measures by targeting houses that have a high probability of becoming and remaining vacant.

  • The externality of vacant houses: The case of Toshima municipality, Tokyo, Japan 2020

    Sadayuki, T., Kanayama, Y. and Arimura, T. H. Review of Regional Studies, 50(2). | Media coverage: Waseda Weekly (早稲田ウィークリー)

    Abstract

    The Japanese housing market has experienced a rapid increase in the number of vacant housing units due to regulatory obstacles and a decreasing population. Abandoned vacant houses can cause negative externalities in the surrounding neighborhood, due to illegal dumping of garbage, increased risks of arson, and building collapse. Few empirical studies exist that focus on the negative externalities of vacant houses, because of data limitations. This paper investigates these negative externalities by using a complete field survey conducted in the Toshima municipality, one of 23 wards in the Tokyo prefecture. We find that a vacant (single-family) house devalues nearby rental prices by 1–2 percent, on average. Vacant houses with property defects cause greater spillover effects. Addressing dilapidated vacant housing with overgrown vegetation and combustible materials would likely produce annual tax gains greater than one million yen (approximately nine thousand U.S. dollars) per vacant housing unit. Given the substantial number of existing vacant houses, local governments should identify the types of vacant houses causing the most severe negative externalities based on empirical assessment and implement efficient countermeasures to address the issue.

  • Lot shape matters a lot: The geometry of urban land market frictions

    Joint with Aiba, I. and Tanaka, K. | Draft coming soon

    Abstract

    This study examines whether the geometric characteristics of land lots can be sources of frictions in the land market. We first propose a search-and-matching model of the land market that links shape irregularity to vacancy duration and the likelihood that a lot is put on the market. To test the theoretical predictions, we apply a Cox proportional hazards model for the duration analysis and a linear probability model for the listing decisions analysis to a rich set of GIS polygon data on vacant lots from a satellite city of Tokyo. We find that shape irregularity — measured as the areal deviation from a minimum bounding rectangle — is associated with longer vacancy and lower probability of being listed for sale. The results suggest that lot shape is not merely a passive spatial trait but a structural source of land market frictions, which could hinder the efficient supply of housing in high-demand areas.
